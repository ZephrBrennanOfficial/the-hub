# Government Spending & Debt Trajectory

**Author:** Zephr Brennan  
**Date:** August 2026  
**Location:** Auckland, Aotearoa NZ

---

## Overview

New Zealand's core Crown spending has grown significantly over the past decade. This analysis tracks revenue, expenses, net debt, and per-capita spending to ask: is the spending sustainable, and what are we getting for it?

## What the Data Says

**1. Spending growth outpaced population growth significantly.** Per-capita spending rose from ~$15,748 in 2015 to ~$25,832 in 2025 — a 64% increase. CPI inflation over that period was approximately 25%. Real per-capita spending grew by roughly 31%.

**2. COVID created a structural shift, not a temporary spike.** Expenses jumped from $87.1B (2019) to $114.6B (2021) — a $27.5B increase. By 2025, expenses had not returned to pre-COVID trend lines. The spending base reset upward.

**3. Revenue is volatile but debt is sticky.** The $9.3B surplus in 2022 was driven by unexpected tax revenue (corporate tax + GST on inflation). By 2024, that reversed to an $8.3B deficit. Net debt climbed back to 26.8% of GDP after briefly dipping to 21.5%.

**4. Interest costs are rising.** As debt has grown, so has the cost of servicing it. Finance costs rose from $2.8B in 2019 to approximately $5.1B in 2025 — money that doesn't fund services.

## Honest Questions

- If per-capita spending grew 64% in a decade, are public services 64% better? What would the evidence look like?
- The COVID spending was justified as emergency response. But the spending base never reset. Was that deliberate or accidental?
- Net debt at 26.8% of GDP is low by international standards. But the trajectory matters. Are we borrowing for investment or consumption?
- What is the measurable return on the additional $51B in annual spending since 2019?

---

## Limitations

- Crown financial statements include accounting adjustments that can distort year-on-year comparisons.
- Population figures use mid-year estimates; actual spending occurs across the year.
- Net debt figures reflect Treasury's chosen methodology — gross debt tells a different (higher) story.
- This analysis does not assess *what* the spending was spent on — only the aggregate trajectory.
